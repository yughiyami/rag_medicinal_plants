\begin{abstract}
The vast existing literature on Peruvian medicinal plants suffers from a serious fragmentation problem. Being scattered across numerous repositories, extracting real pharmacological evidence is costly. To address this, we propose \sirca{} (System for Intelligent Retrieval and Corrective Answers), a bilingual architecture designed to unify this evidence through corrective techniques. Our solution fuses dense and lexical (BM25) search using \emph{Reciprocal Rank Fusion}, then applies a cross-encoder reranker that contributes in a directionally consistent way to answer fidelity. A CRAG-style router manages final quality. Our central empirical contribution is not a single retrieval score but a scoring finding: we show that the default within-batch normalization suppresses the CRAG corrective routes, and that replacing it with a per-document absolute transformation of the reranker logits is what makes the fallback toward external search reachable. We use \emph{absolute transformation} rather than \emph{calibration} throughout, since we do not verify that the transformed scores match empirical correctness frequencies. We evaluate the architecture on a corpus of 16{,}486 collected records, of which 6{,}481 documents (32{,}569 chunks, 91 species) are retrievable, using a bilingual benchmark of 50 questions with author-validated reference answers and attaining competitive metrics (Fidelity of $0{,}670$, MRR of $0{,}897$).

\keywords{Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \and Peruvian Ethnobotany \and Large Language Models (LLM) \and Hybrid Retrieval \and Natural Language Processing (NLP)}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

We know that Peru has around 25{,}000 plant species, and at least between 1{,}400 and 5{,}000 have some degree of pharmacological evidence~\cite{bussmann2006traditional,bussmann2011toxicity}. However, if a researcher wants to track down specific data about \textit{Uncaria tomentosa} or \textit{Croton lechleri}, they will soon run into the central problem: the data is not in one place. Publications are scattered across all of PubMed, as well as local biodiversity databases and international records~\cite{lock2016bioactive,gonzales2012ethnobiology}. Putting together a systematic review under these conditions takes too long and leaves gaps.

Lately, Large Language Models (\emph{LLMs}) have attracted interest for summarizing large volumes of text. By injecting context through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis2020retrieval}, the model appears to ``know'' the subject. In medical practice, however, this hits a wall: the system often pulls in paragraphs that are useless and, worse, invents pharmacological conclusions that look true but lack support~\cite{gao2024retrieval,ji2023survey}. Not even models trained specifically on medical text are spared from this~\cite{lee2020biobert,gu2021domain}. To get around this obstacle, Yan et al.~\cite{yan2024crag} published Corrective RAG (CRAG). The idea behind CRAG is simple but effective: measure whether what you just retrieved is actually useful; if it is not, the model asks to rewrite the query or goes to search the web.

The central contribution we present here is \sirca{} (System for Intelligent Retrieval and Corrective Answers). What we did was take that CRAG concept and adapt it to handle the enormous botanical complexity of Peru. We had to make four strong design decisions. First, we decided not to marry a single retrieval method; we combined \texttt{multilingual-e5-base} vectors~\cite{wang2024e5} with classic BM25 keyword search~\cite{robertson2009bm25} using RRF~\cite{cormack2009rrf}. Second, we added a \emph{cross-encoder}~\cite{nogueira2019passage} because we realized that basic bi-encoders skip over subtle technical details. Third, we implemented a routing safeguard: if confidence drops, we block the generator. Fourth, we forced the model (via CoT~\cite{wei2022chain}) to extract the data, verify it, and only then write the answer, indicating which passage it used. All of this left us with five specific contributions: a bilingual QA architecture for our flora; a massive curated corpus of 16{,}486 articles spanning 100 species; 50 questions we validated ourselves; a way of measuring fidelity that combines lexical with semantic matching; and finally, direct tests with multiple models.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{RAG Ecosystem and Corrective Variants.} The paradigm was introduced by Lewis et al.~\cite{lewis2020retrieval}, who showed that conditioning generation on retrieved documents improves answer quality; Gao et al.~\cite{gao2024retrieval} later surveyed the variants that subsequently emerged. Among them, the corrective proposal of Yan et al.~\cite{yan2024crag} is especially relevant to this work, since it evaluates retrieval quality before generation. We adopt this same philosophy, although instead of training a dedicated evaluator we use the \emph{logits} of an already-trained \emph{cross-encoder} directly. Recent empirical evidence supports this design choice: Gangavarapu et al.~\cite{gangavarapu2025corrective} report that corrective RAG consistently improves accuracy over naive retrieval on question-answering benchmarks. Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag} also allows the LLM to decide when it needs additional information by generating special tokens, but it requires modifying the LLM internally, which exceeds the computational budget of this project.

\textbf{Reliability of Medical RAG.} In the medical domain, retrieval is not universally beneficial. Xie et al.~\cite{xie2026retrievalhurts} show, on MedQA-USMLE, that several RAG configurations underperform a pure-LLM baseline because retrieval injects distractors roughly as often as it supplies missing evidence---a dual effect that motivates confidence-aware routing rather than unconditional retrieval. Wo{\l}k~\cite{wolk2025clinical} likewise frames hallucination mitigation as the central requirement for clinical decision support. These findings justify our emphasis on a strict faithfulness metric and on a corrective router able to abstain when the retrieved evidence is insufficient.

\textbf{Hybrid Search and Reranking.} There is consistent evidence that combining dense and sparse retrieval outperforms either approach alone~\cite{karpukhin2020dense}, as each closes gaps that the other leaves open~\cite{sinha2026lexicalsemantic}. We therefore use RRF~\cite{cormack2009rrf} to combine \texttt{e5-base}~\cite{wang2024e5} with BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25}, a method that does not require labeled data to calibrate combination weights; we note that Bruch et al.~\cite{bruch2023fusion} show convex-combination fusion can surpass RRF in some settings, a refinement we leave open. Fusion is particularly valuable for the long-tail taxonomic terms that pervade our corpus, where entity-aware hierarchical retrieval yields substantial recall gains~\cite{peng2026hce}. The fused pool is subsequently refined by a \emph{cross-encoder}~\cite{nogueira2019passage}; consistent with our ablation study, Elkiran and Rasheed~\cite{elkiran2026rerankerpairings} identify the retriever--reranker pairing as the decisive quality--efficiency trade-off in RAG pipelines.

\textbf{Evaluation Methodology.} We use the RAGAs framework~\cite{es2023ragas}, which is widely adopted as a de facto standard, and complement the evaluation with an \emph{LLM-as-judge} mechanism~\cite{zheng2023mtbench} (detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:llm_judge}) that provides an independent cross-check.

\textbf{Regional, Multilingual and Domain-Specific RAG.} Xiong et al.~\cite{xiong2024mirage}, with their MIRAGE project, showed that RAG outperforms models without retrieval on medical tasks, and Aljohani and Alsanoosy~\cite{aljohani2026hybridmedical} confirm the benefit of hybrid retrieval for medical question answering specifically. Bilingual operation is central to our setting: Chirkova et al.~\cite{chirkova2024multilingual} characterize the components required for a well-performing multilingual RAG pipeline, while low-resource medical deployments---such as medication question answering over Yor\`ub\'a drug labels~\cite{adebesin2026yoruba}---show that hybrid signals become indispensable when data is scarce. For medicinal-plant knowledge specifically, Riddhi et al.~\cite{riddhi2025indianplants} integrate Indian flora through a RAG pipeline, but without corrective routing or a faithfulness-oriented evaluation. In the Latin American context, Monroy Barrios et al.~\cite{monroy2026finetuning} fine-tuned a model on Peruvian flora texts, achieving a substantial reduction in perplexity, although the acquired knowledge remains encapsulated in the model's parameters and is not externally auditable. We favor RAG precisely because it allows the source document of each claim to be traced, an important requirement in the pharmacological domain. As a complementary architecture, Collanqui et al.~\cite{collanqui2026cagrag} contrasted RAG with \emph{Cache-Augmented Generation} over a single institutional regulation; we do not compare against it numerically, since its corpus (one document), domain (administrative), and $k{=}1$ retrieval differ too much from ours (16{,}486 articles, 100 species) for an isolated figure to be informative. To the best of our knowledge, based on an exhaustive literature review, no prior work combines a CRAG pipeline with hybrid retrieval applied to Andean medicinal plants.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The full process is split into phases connected to one another. The first thing the system does is read the question and decide what type of query it is. Depending on the verbal patterns we find, we bucket it as \textit{factual}, \textit{exploratory}, or \textit{comparative}; this label travels in the execution trace for explainability and debugging purposes. In an early version of the system, this classification also set a different $\alpha$ per category (factual $\rightarrow$ BM25, exploratory $\rightarrow$ semantic). A held-out $\alpha$ sweep (Section~\ref{sec:wilcoxon}) showed that this per-category weighting, even when properly tuned, does not beat a fixed $\alpha$; we therefore removed it, and retrieval always uses $\alpha{=}0{,}6$.

Once classification is done, parallel retrieval kicks in. On one track runs \texttt{multilingual-e5-base}~\cite{wang2024e5}, turning the text into 768-dimensional vectors that are compared mathematically in FAISS~\cite{johnson2021billion}. On the other track runs BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25}, searching for specific terms the vector might have missed. The two ranked lists are merged by a weighted variant of Reciprocal Rank Fusion~\cite{cormack2009rrf}. Writing $r_d(q,x)$ and $r_s(q,x)$ for the rank of chunk $x$ under dense and sparse retrieval, the fused score is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rrf}
\mathrm{RRF}_\alpha(q,x) \;=\; \frac{\alpha}{k + r_d(q,x) + 1} \;+\; \frac{1-\alpha}{k + r_s(q,x) + 1},
\end{equation}
with $k{=}60$, $\alpha{=}0{,}6$, and a term omitted when the chunk is absent from that list. Note that $\alpha$ weights the two \emph{rank-reciprocal} contributions; it is not a convex combination of similarity scores. The top 30 fused chunks form the candidate pool. This parameterisation has a consequence we quantify in Sec.~\ref{sec:pool}.

That is where \texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-12-v2}~\cite{nogueira2019passage} comes into play. This module takes the 30 texts and the original question, and reads them together to refine the relevance score. We keep only the top 10. At this point, the system evaluates the maximum score obtained. We pass that score through a sigmoid function to flatten it and decide: if it exceeds $0{,}60$ (and the rest of the documents are not deficient), we approve the batch. If it falls between $0{,}30$ and $0{,}60$, we raise a ``refinement'' alert. And if it is below $0{,}30$, the system abandons the local index and signals the external-search fallback. Section~\ref{sec:crag_validation} quantifies why this choice of transformation is what makes the corrective branches reachable.

Finally, if the batch was approved, we take the top 5 excerpts and pass them to the DeepSeek V4-Flash model~\cite{deepseek2026v4}\footnote{Accessed through the official DeepSeek API (\url{https://api.deepseek.com}), under the model identifier \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash}.}. We do not simply demand the answer outright; we impose a logical flow of extracting, comparing, and only then drafting. And most critically: its ability to infer data not present in those paragraphs is explicitly blocked.

\section{Corpus and Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

\textbf{On the data handled.} The corpus was assembled from eight repositories: the bibliographic sources PubMed, Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar and CrossRef, and the structured databases GBIF, PeruNPDB, WFO and COCONUT. We targeted 100 plants from southern Peru and removed duplicate articles by cross-checking DOIs, retaining 16{,}486 unique records. It is important to separate what was \emph{collected} from what is \emph{retrievable}: only records whose text mentions one of the catalogued species are indexed, which yields the retrieval index actually used in every experiment below --- 6{,}481 documents split into 32{,}569 chunks covering 91 species. What is indexed is title plus abstract, not full text (median $1{,}461$ characters per document); chunks are 512 \emph{characters} with 64 characters of overlap, which is a median of 51 words. Of the eight repositories, the four bibliographic ones contribute all indexed material (PubMed 1{,}002 documents, CrossRef 227, Europe PMC 46, Semantic Scholar 3 before lifting a per-species cap); the four structured databases (GBIF, PeruNPDB, WFO, COCONUT) were queried for taxonomic and phytochemical validation but contribute no indexed chunks, since their records are field-structured rather than prose. Nine of the 100 species have no indexed literature (\textit{Adesmia spinosissima}, \textit{Berberis lutea}, among other high-altitude taxa); we keep them deliberately as a stress-test family for the corrective router (Sec.~\ref{sec:crag_validation}). The corpus and the index are released in the public repository.

\textbf{On the experiments.} We ourselves created a set of 50 questions in Spanish and English (mixing concept questions, comparisons, and hard facts) about 12 species, and validated what the ideal answer should be. To avoid relying on that alone, we set up the evaluation on three fronts: the 50 base questions; an extended set covering 30 additional species, to test whether quality degrades outside the verified subset; and a set of out-of-distribution probes designed to force the corrective branches to activate.

As for how we measure success, we follow standard parameters. On the retrieval side we use Context Precision, Recall, MRR, and NDCG. On the generation side, we use BERTScore F1~\cite{zhang2020bertscore}. And to make sure the model is not hallucinating medical data, we devised a ``Fidelity'' metric that blends semantic evaluation with literal text matches. We also ran simulations removing pieces of the system step by step (what we call ablation) to test what happened.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

On the 50-query, 12-species human-verified benchmark, \sirca{} achieves a Context Recall of $0{,}485$, an MRR of $0{,}897$, an NDCG@10 of $0{,}887$ and a Fidelity of $0{,}670$ (Table~\ref{tab:full_results}, \texttt{full} column). The MRR indicates that, for the large majority of queries, the most relevant document lands in the first or second rank position. We do not compare these values numerically against published prior work: the only related work we identified in Spanish (Collanqui et al.~\cite{collanqui2026cagrag}, Sec.~\ref{sec:related}) evaluates a domain, corpus, and retrieval strategy too different from ours — a single institutional regulation with $k{=}1$ retrieval — for a direct quantitative comparison to be valid.

Generation metrics are also solid: BERTScore F1 of $0{,}820$ (\texttt{roberta-large}), Semantic Similarity of $0{,}770$, and Answer Relevancy of $0{,}999$. The exception is Entity Coverage ($0{,}428$), which lags behind the rest. We manually inspected the lowest-scoring queries. The generator's final step is highly selective: it tends to mention a single compound or plant per statement (whichever has the most textual support) and ignores secondary entities that do appear in the reference paragraph. As a result, automated metrics that demand entity exhaustiveness penalize it, even though the answer is technically correct. Fidelity of $0{,}670$ is the $n{=}80$ headline value; its $35\,\%$ lexical term deliberately penalizes paraphrasing of chemical formulas and taxonomic names to reduce the risk of pharmacological hallucination. On the 50-query subset used for the generator comparison, where the two components were measured separately, DeepSeek scores $0{,}613$ semantic and $0{,}587$ lexical, which combine to the $0{,}604$ reported in Table~\ref{tab:multi_llm} (Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity_weight}).

\textbf{Retrieval generalization.} Table~\ref{tab:extended_retrieval} shows that retrieval quality is preserved when the evaluation is widened from the 12 human-verified species to 30 further species sampled from the catalogue: MRR moves from $0{,}897$ to $0{,}883$, NDCG@10 from $0{,}887$ to $0{,}867$ and Context Recall from $0{,}485$ to $0{,}489$. The comparison splits along metric families rather than favouring one generator outright. DeepSeek leads on Entity Coverage ($0{,}428$ vs $0{,}365$, $p{=}0{,}012$) and, without reaching significance, on Answer Relevancy ($0{,}999$ vs $0{,}979$, $p{=}0{,}180$) and Semantic Similarity ($p{=}0{,}931$). A decomposition of the Fidelity metric locates Gemma's advantage more precisely, and corrects the explanation we first offered. Recomputing Fidelity across the full range of the semantic/lexical weighting (Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity_weight}) shows that Gemma's margin is \emph{largest} under a purely semantic weighting ($+0{,}108$) and \emph{smallest}, and not significant, under a purely lexical one ($+0{,}035$, $p{=}0{,}249$). The advantage is therefore driven by the cross-encoder component that judges whether each sentence is supported by the context, not by verbatim reuse of context wording as we initially assumed. Decomposed, Gemma scores $0{,}721$ semantic and $0{,}622$ lexical against DeepSeek's $0{,}613$ and $0{,}587$.

Two architectural points follow: (i)~the \sirca{} pipeline tolerates a change of generator without additional tuning, and (ii)~the choice of generator matters for grounding specifically, not for generation quality in general. In this run both generators produced a usable answer for every query ($0/50$ failures each), after two DeepSeek queries that initially returned empty content were regenerated; Limitation~(vii) documents the mechanism.

\subsection{Cross-Validation}
\label{sec:llm_judge}

To test whether the Fidelity metric tracks grounding as intended, we applied the \emph{LLM-as-judge} technique~\cite{zheng2023mtbench}, having Gemma-4-31B decompose each DeepSeek answer into individual factual claims and report how many are explicitly supported by the provided context --- a claim-level rubric rather than a single holistic score, to avoid a pile-up of perfect scores. The evaluator comes from a different model family than the generator it grades. The qualitative result is favourable: on the final index, 42 of the 48 gradable answers had every claim supported ($88\,\%$; the earlier holistic 0--4 rubric gave $86\,\%$ and the first claim-level run $74\,\%$). Even so, that rate leaves little variance for a formal correlation. Across three attempts the correlation between the judge's score and the algorithmic metric is null in every case: $r{=}0{,}056$ ($p{=}0{,}70$) for the holistic 0--4 rubric, $r{=}{-}0{,}167$ ($p{=}0{,}27$) for the claim-level rubric, and $r{=}0{,}039$ ($p{=}0{,}79$, $n{=}48$) for the claim-level rubric recomputed on the final index, where 42 of 48 gradable answers had every claim supported. Three rubric designs over two indices reproduce the same null result, so we do not attribute it to a ceiling artifact of any single scale: the LLM judge does not quantitatively validate Fidelity.

The scale gap is informative in itself. The judge averages $0{,}977$ (normalized) against the metric's $0{,}593$ on the same answers, because the metric is deliberately unforgiving toward medical paraphrase: we prefer a low score to endorsing a phrase that reads as similar but could carry a clinical error. No automated judge substitutes for a pharmacist's review; the exercise works as a coarse filter for unsupported answers, not as a quantitative validation of Fidelity, which after three attempts we did not establish.

\subsection{Sensitivity of the Fidelity Weighting}
\label{sec:fidelity_weight}

Fidelity combines a semantic term (sentence-level cross-encoder support) with a lexical
term (content-word overlap) under a fixed $65/35$ weighting. Those weights were set a
priori, so we report how the conclusions depend on them. Sweeping the semantic weight
$w$ from $0$ to $1$ in steps of $0{,}05$ and recomputing the DeepSeek-vs-Gemma
comparison at every point (Table~\ref{tab:weight_sweep}) gives a single crossing: the
Gemma advantage is significant for every $w \geq 0{,}35$ and not significant below it.
The reported operating point $w{=}0{,}65$ therefore sits inside a broad significant
region rather than at a favourable singularity, but the finding is weight-dependent at
the lexical end and we state it as such.

The sweep also corrects our own reading of the cross-generator result. We had attributed

\subsection{Where Hybrid Fusion Contributes}
\label{sec:pool}

Because \texttt{dense\_only} and \texttt{full} return an identical Top-10 on every query
(Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}), the reported benchmark cannot establish an incremental
benefit of hybrid retrieval after reranking. The cause is arithmetic rather than
empirical, and follows from Eq.~\ref{eq:rrf}. With $k{=}60$, a pool of $30$ and
$\alpha{=}0{,}6$, the dense document at rank 30 scores $0{,}6/(60{+}31)=0{,}006593$
while a sparse-only document at rank 1 scores $0{,}4/(60{+}2)=0{,}006452$. The former is
larger, so \emph{no} document unique to BM25 can enter the pool while 30 dense
candidates exist: we confirmed $0$ such documents across all queries in both indices.
The crossing point is $\alpha^\ast = (k{+}31)/(2k{+}33) = 0{,}5948$; below it BM25
contributes documents, above it only ranking evidence through the agreement bonus.

Measuring the configurations on the candidate pool \emph{before} the cross-encoder makes
the contribution visible (Table~\ref{tab:pool}). Hybrid fusion improves ranking over
dense-only (NDCG@10 $0{,}711 \rightarrow 0{,}755$; C.\ Precision $0{,}517 \rightarrow
0{,}541$) while Recall@30 is identical by the argument above. The honest claim is
therefore narrower than the one we first made: at $\alpha{=}0{,}6$ the lexical branch
re-ranks the dense pool and does not extend it, and the benefit does not survive the
cross-encoder. Lowering $\alpha$ below $0{,}5948$, or fusing before truncation, is the
experiment that would let the lexical branch contribute coverage; we leave it open.

\subsection{Findings from Taking the System Apart}

Removing pieces one by one (Table~\ref{tab:full_results}) revealed three useful truths. First, the reranking module is consistently associated with better Fidelity. Removing it lowers Fidelity by $4{,}5\,\%$ relative (from $0{,}670$ to $0{,}640$); the direction repeated across all six runs of this experiment, but the paired Wilcoxon test over $n{=}80$ queries does not reach conventional two-sided significance ($p{=}0{,}082$; $p{=}0{,}041$ one-sided; Sec.~\ref{sec:wilcoxon}), and $49$ of the $80$ queries favour \texttt{full} --- see Limitation~(ix). Second, keyword search alone (\texttt{sparse\_only}) shows a counterintuitive trade-off: it achieves the best C.Prec. ($0{,}465$) and C.Recall ($0{,}525$) of the five configurations, but the worst MRR ($0{,}867$) and NDCG@10 ($0{,}832$) --- BM25 brings more relevant documents into the 30-item pool, but without the dense semantic signal it orders them worse before reranking. None of these differences reach per-query significance (Sec.~\ref{sec:wilcoxon}). Third, \texttt{dense\_only} produces exactly the same Top-10 as \texttt{full} across all 50 queries ($n_{\neq}{=}0$) on both indices. Section~\ref{sec:pool} shows this is arithmetic rather than empirical: at $\alpha{=}0{,}6$ no BM25-unique document can enter the 30-document pool, so the lexical branch re-ranks the dense pool instead of extending it. Looking at the clock, retrieval takes us barely $\sim$1\,810\,ms to process on CPU, and drafting takes between 10 and 30 seconds using the DeepSeek server.

\textbf{Note on runs and the absolute value of Fidelity.} DeepSeek's Fidelity in the cross-LLM comparison (Table~\ref{tab:multi_llm}, $0{,}604$) differs from \texttt{full}'s Fidelity in Table~\ref{tab:full_results} ($0{,}670$) because the two come from different runs and different query sets: Table~\ref{tab:multi_llm} is the paired run with Gemma over the 50 benchmark queries, while Table~\ref{tab:full_results} is a dedicated ablation run over $n{=}80$. The one result reproducible across runs — and the one that supports the article's conclusion — is the \emph{direction} of the differences, not their absolute value, and (as documented above) not always their formal significance either.

\subsection{The Within-Batch Normalization Defect}
\label{sec:crag_validation}

The initial implementation contained a normalization defect. The evaluator rescaled each batch of retrieved blocks to the $[0,1]$ interval by min--max normalization. This makes the threshold relative to the batch: the best document of every query is mapped to $1{,}0$ regardless of its absolute relevance, so the accept branch is reached even when no retrieved text is relevant. Sec.~\ref{sec:crag_validation} quantifies how much this shifts the routing distribution. We replaced it with a per-document logistic transformation of the raw reranker logit, $s_\sigma = \sigma(s)$, which is absolute in the sense that it does not depend on the other documents retrieved for the same query. We set the operating thresholds at $0{,}60$ to accept and $0{,}30$ to refine. We deliberately avoid calling this a \emph{calibration}: a calibrated score would have to match empirical correctness frequencies, which we do not verify here.

To verify that the corrective branches fire as designed, we assembled 27 out-of-distribution probes in three families: taxa absent from the index, queries outside the botanical domain, and lexically degraded strings. Table~\ref{tab:crag_stress} shows how the algorithm defended itself.

There was a single bypass with \textit{Senecio canescens}, caused by the engine retrieving data from phylogenetically related taxa. On the in-domain benchmark itself (50 queries), the absolute transformation does not always yield \textsc{accept}: $35/50$ route to \textsc{accept}, $5/50$ to \textsc{refine}, and $10/50$ to \textsc{web\_search}. We interpret these $15$ corrective activations as correct behavior rather than false negatives: they are disproportionately concentrated in \textit{exploratory} and \textit{comparative} queries ($12/15$, $80\%$, versus $51\%$ among accepted queries), categories that by design call for synthesis across multiple sources rather than a single fact, and are therefore more prone to genuinely ambiguous retrieval. We prefer the system to declare uncertainty in those cases rather than fabricate an answer. In short, replacing within-batch normalization with the per-document absolute transformation makes the corrective route reachable both on out-of-distribution input and on low-confidence in-domain queries, which the batch-relative rule had rendered largely indistinguishable from nominal behaviour (Table~\ref{tab:norm_compare}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

When processing herbal-medicine queries, we face heterogeneous structures. A user might ask for ``common names'' while concatenating a rigorous academic term such as \textit{Uncaria tomentosa}. As we document, lexical search is unbeatable for capturing Latin names, but the hybrid method is justified because the \emph{cross-encoder} steps in to weed out results that lack semantic cohesion. Additionally, natively handling multiple languages through the vector space mitigated considerable computational barriers.

Monroy Barrios et al.~\cite{monroy2026finetuning} follow a different route, fine-tuning Llama on Andean texts. That yields fluent answers, but the provenance of a stated dosage cannot be traced back to a source document. Our path with RAG keeps the data outside, auditable, with a traceable reference number. We believe the obvious future lies in merging both worlds: fine-tuning a model and, on top of that, adding a protective RAG layer.

\textbf{Availability and limitations.} The complete code is released in a public repository (URL withheld for double-blind review), together with the 50-question bilingual benchmark and its author-validated reference answers, the retrieval index, and the per-query JSON outputs from which every table and figure in this paper is derived. The experiment runners, their invocation, and the mapping from each script to the result it produces are documented in the repository README rather than in this manuscript; an interactive demo (URL withheld) exposes the retrieved fragments with their DOI/PMID and the routing decision per query. We list below the current limitations so readers can correctly gauge the scope of the results: (i)~the human-verified \emph{benchmark} covers 12 of the 91 indexed species (100 catalogued); retrieval was verified on 42 species in total (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}) and routing on the 9 species with no indexed data (Sec.~\ref{sec:crag_validation}), but full answer-quality evaluation over the remaining 58 species requires building additional reference answers; (ii)~current reference answers were authored by the authors themselves; although the Fidelity metric was cross-checked against an independent LLM-judge (Sec.~\ref{sec:llm_judge}), validation by an external pharmacognosy or ethnobotany specialist is still pending, and that is the first requirement before any clinical or production use; (iii)~for the central comparison (\texttt{full} vs \texttt{no\_reranker}; Sec.~\ref{sec:wilcoxon}) the reranker's contribution to Fidelity is directionally consistent but does not reach two-sided significance ($p{=}0{,}082$, $n{=}80$; see limitation ix); moreover, extending the Fidelity test to all five ablation configurations requires re-running the LLM for each one and remains future work; (iv)~hybrid Fidelity (65\,\% semantic + 35\,\% lexical) was cross-checked against an LLM judge under three rubric/index combinations and none yielded a significant correlation ($r{=}0{,}056$, $p{=}0{,}70$; $r{=}{-}0{,}167$, $p{=}0{,}27$; $r{=}0{,}039$, $p{=}0{,}79$; Sec.~\ref{sec:llm_judge}), and the Gemma-vs-DeepSeek conclusion is weight-dependent below $w{=}0{,}35$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:fidelity_weight}). We therefore treat Fidelity as an experimental proxy for grounding rather than an established factuality measure; correlation with expert human judgments remains open; (v)~robustness across LLMs was verified with a second generator (Gemma-4-31B, served through SambaNova); extending that verification to other families (Llama, Qwen, Mistral) would strengthen the generality claim; (vi)~the operating thresholds ($\sigma_{\mathrm{acc}}{=}0{,}60$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}{=}0{,}30$) were taken from the logistic curve rather than tuned; the sweep in Sec.~\ref{sec:crag_validation} shows they sit on a plateau ($[0{,}50,\,0{,}85]$ gives the same corrective behaviour), so $0{,}60$ is defensible but not distinguished, and locating an optimum would need a broader out-of-distribution set; (vii)~the generator returns empty content on a minority of queries, and the mechanism is worth stating precisely because our first diagnosis was wrong. \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash} emits an intermediate \texttt{reasoning\_content} field that is billed against the \texttt{max\_tokens} budget; when the reasoning consumes the whole budget the reply arrives with \texttt{finish\_reason}\,=\,\texttt{length} and an empty answer. The reasoning length is stochastic rather than deterministic: the same prompt, at temperature $0$ and a 1{,}500-token budget, returned a complete answer on one call and an empty one on the next. Retrying therefore does help, but only probabilistically and at the cost of another call; raising the budget addresses the cause, and the two affected queries in the cross-LLM run needed 3{,}000 and 6{,}000 tokens respectively. With a budget that escalates on empty content, none of the 160 generations of the Fidelity experiment came back blank. A separate transient DNS failure, which the HTTP client did not retry because it caught only HTTP-level errors and not socket-level ones, cost $19/50$ responses in an earlier run and is fixed with retry and exponential backoff. A related metric defect surfaced while auditing these failures: \texttt{bertscore\_lite} assigns Semantic Similarity of $0{,}945$ and $0{,}961$ to \emph{empty} answers, where BERTScore F1, Entity Coverage, Fidelity and Answer Relevancy all correctly return $0$. That column is therefore inflated whenever answers are lost, and should not be read without checking the blank count; (viii)~absolute values of the generation metrics depend on the point in time at which the commercial DeepSeek API was accessed, so exact reproduction may differ from the values reported here; the \emph{direction} of the reranker$\rightarrow$Fidelity effect held stable across all six runs performed during this work, but \emph{formal significance} did not (Limitation~ix), so only direction should be read as stable across runs; (ix)~the Wilcoxon test on Fidelity (\texttt{full} vs \texttt{no\_reranker}) has now been run six times over the course of this work, each on a code or index state that differed by a fixed bug or by the retrieval index: $p{=}0{,}016$ and $p{=}0{,}00023$ ($n{=}50$, with the per-category $\alpha$ classifier still active and before an $\alpha$-restore-order fix in the agent graph); $p{=}0{,}110$ ($n{=}50$, classifier removed); $p{=}0{,}028$ ($n{=}80$); $p{=}0{,}160$ ($n{=}80$, after fixing the string-hash non-determinism in \texttt{\_refine\_query()}); and $p{=}0{,}082$ two-sided / $p{=}0{,}041$ one-sided ($n{=}80$) on the final index. Three of the six reach two-sided significance and three do not, and the two run on the fully-fixed pipeline before the re-index did not. Enlarging the sample from 50 to 80 did not by itself produce stability. The direction is the stable part: \texttt{full} exceeded \texttt{no\_reranker} in all six runs ($0{,}534{>}0{,}467$; $0{,}566{>}0{,}465$; $0{,}517{>}0{,}469$; $0{,}562{>}0{,}514$; $0{,}554{>}0{,}526$; $0{,}670{>}0{,}640$), and in the final run $49$ of the $80$ queries favour \texttt{full} with a mean per-query difference of $+0{,}030$ --- a majority, not a uniform improvement. We therefore report the reranker's contribution to Fidelity as a consistent directional signal that does not reach the two-sided significance level this paper uses elsewhere; (x)~the bilingual claim is asymmetric: retrieval shows no significant difference between the Spanish and English subsets, but grounded generation is measurably weaker in Spanish (Fidelity $0{,}468$ vs $0{,}680$, $p{=}0{,}001$; Sec.~\ref{sec:language}), because the indexed literature is almost entirely in English; raising Spanish-language coverage of the corpus is the direct remedy and is not attempted here.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Throughout this manuscript we outlined \sirca{}'s architecture. We integrated parallel retrieval methods, incorporated a strict reranker, established an absolute per-document scoring rule for the corrective router, and forced the model to use sequential reasoning before synthesizing bilingual botanical outputs. Our contribution is not a record retrieval score --- on the 50-query benchmark the system attains a Context Recall of $0{,}485$, an MRR of $0{,}897$ and a Fidelity of $0{,}670$, competitive values that are not the focus --- but the corrective design and its scoring: we show that within-batch normalization raises out-of-distribution acceptance from $5/27$ to $16/27$ probes and removes the categorical rejection of off-domain queries ($0/10$ accepted versus $4/10$), and that a per-document absolute transformation restores that rejection while still routing low-confidence in-domain queries to the corrective branches. The reranker is associated with better Fidelity in a directionally consistent way ($-4{,}5\,\%$ relative when removed in the final run), and \texttt{full} exceeded \texttt{no\_reranker} in all six runs performed during this work, but the effect does not reach robust statistical significance: the two-sided Wilcoxon $p$ ranged from $0{,}00023$ to $0{,}160$ across runs and the final run gives $p{=}0{,}082$ ($p{=}0{,}041$ one-sided), with $49$ of $80$ queries favouring \texttt{full} (Limitation ix). We report this finding honestly as a directional signal, not as a confirmed effect. A held-out $\alpha$ sweep also led us to simplify the architecture: the per-category weight the query classifier assigned to retrieval did not beat a fixed $\alpha$ even when properly tuned, so we removed it from the production system. Cross-validation with a second generator (Gemma-4-31B) confirmed that the architecture tolerates swapping the LLM without design changes. Two of the five generation metrics differ significantly between generators — Fidelity, in Gemma's favour, and Entity Coverage, in DeepSeek's — while BERTScore F1 separates them by only $0{,}014$ and Semantic Similarity and Answer Relevancy show no significant difference. We observed firsthand how standard software's default behaviors compromise corrective pipelines if one does not intervene directly in the mechanics of empirical normalization. As a corollary, the future trajectory is clear: it is imperative to set up panels with biologists to audit the answers under strict professional scrutiny~\cite{heinrich2020ethnopharmacology}, extend the scope of our geographic tests, and, on a more ambitious scale, run parametric modifications on the LLM's weights to internalize botanical ontology prior to document analysis.
